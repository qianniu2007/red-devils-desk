How it was made

A project about
counting properly.

This page is the part most course projects skip: the brief, the plan, the design decisions, where the numbers come from, and an honest account of which parts were made with AI and which were not.

1. The brief I set myself

My first submission for this course was a replica of the course website, and it deserved the reaction it got: it explained HTML, CSS and JavaScript back to the person who had just taught them. It was a summary, not a project. So the second attempt starts from a different question — what do I actually want to look up, and what does the course site not do?

I follow Manchester United. What I want, and cannot easily find in one place, is the numbers: every season's shape, who actually played, which trophy came from which year — without a video player starting, a cookie banner, or an advert covering the table.

  • Goal: a small, fast, checkable data site about one club — three datasets, five ways to interrogate them.
  • Audience: someone like me, 18–25, on a phone in a dorm with a weak connection, who knows the club but not the records, and who wants an answer in under ten seconds.
  • Success: a stranger can open it, find any season's points and position, and explain the post-2013 drop with a number rather than a feeling.
  • Explicitly out of scope: news, transfers, opinion columns, comments, accounts, a back end, a framework.

2. The plan, before any code

One page of planning, one sitemap, then wireframes on paper. The wireframes are not reproduced here because they are pencil on the back of a lecture handout, but the structure they produced is this:

index.html           the pitch: 20 titles, 68 trophies, four counters,
                     a nine-moment timeline, four ways into the data
legends.html         24 players, the XI builder, the sortable index
seasons.html         33 Premier League seasons: chart, era filter, table
trophies.html        10 competitions, every winning year, by decade
quiz.html            10 questions, each correction links to its source
about.html           the brief, the design system, the sources, the AI note

assets/css/site.css  one stylesheet, tokens first
assets/js/data.js    all of the data, in one file, no fetch()
assets/js/site.js    mobile menu, reveal, the counters
assets/js/           legends.js  seasons.js  trophies.js  quiz.js
                     one file per page, no bundler

Two planning decisions mattered more than the rest. First, the data had to live in one file, because a project that repeats its numbers in five pages will eventually contradict itself. Second, every interactive page had to have a text version — the table under the chart exists for exactly that reason, and it is not a fallback for a broken site but the other half of the same information.

3. The design system

The reference is the Apple Sports match card on iOS: a dark surface washed in the club's colour, a crest, a large number where the score would be, and tables that look like league standings. Everything is a translucent panel over that wash — no flat grey boxes, no drop-shadow cards floating on white.

The 60-30-10 rule, rebuilt for a red site: roughly 60% of the screen is a very dark red, 30% is red glass over it, and the last 10% is the bright club red used as a claim — a title-winning season, a filled position, the primary button. Even the neutral panels are red-tinted rather than grey; the only thing that is not red is the type.

Type: the system UI stack, the same one iOS uses, so the site renders in SF Pro on a phone and Segoe UI on this laptop without downloading anything. Every number is tabular so columns line up; nothing is smaller than 11px.

The crest and the faces are real. The club crest is the actual badge; the 22 player portraits are photographs, not drawings. Both are used under a clear rule, documented in the credit list below: the crest is the club's trademark (fair use on Wikipedia, so it is the first thing I would take down if this ever left a coursework context), while every player photograph is free-licensed — CC BY or CC BY-SA with the author named. Two players, Duncan Edwards and Gary Pallister, have no free-licensed portrait, so they keep a generated initials disc rather than a bad or stolen picture.

One theme, not two. The last version shipped a light and a dark mode; that is two design systems to keep in sync and it halved the attention each one got. Apple Sports' identity is the dark card, so this is a dark-first site and I spent the time on the wash, the panel and the tables instead.

4. Build notes

  • No framework, no build step. Six HTML files, one stylesheet, six small scripts. If it is on GitHub Pages, it is exactly what I wrote.
  • It works from file://. All data is a plain script assigning to window.UTD, so there is no fetch() to break when someone double-clicks the file. (My first submission failed exactly there: the links were local paths, and the professor was right to call them invalid.)
  • Every link is relative and every page exists. The nav, the footer and the quiz corrections are all real files in the repository. Nothing points at a page I did not build.
  • State is small and visible. The XI builder and the quiz keep their state in one object and write it to localStorage; the filter and metric buttons only ever change one property.
  • Accessibility as a constraint, not a decoration. Skip link; one h1 per page; chart marks are focusable with a spoken label; the chart has a table twin; colour is never the only signal; visible focus rings; prefers-reduced-motion disables the reveal. Body text sits at 74–100% white on #160206, which clears 4.5:1.
  • Performance by subtraction. 22 portraits at 320px, one crest SVG, no web fonts, no libraries, no icon package — and every photograph is lazy-loaded. The whole site stays under a megabyte and works with JavaScript on a slow dorm connection.

5. Where the numbers come from

Every figure on this site is checkable, and these are the records I checked against:

  • Season-by-season league positions and points, 1992–93 to 2024–25: the club's own season records, cross-read against the published Premier League tables for each season.
  • Honours and winning years: the club's honours list, including the note that the Community Shield total of 21 includes four shared titles.
  • Appearances and goals: the club's all-time appearance and goalscorer lists. Ryan Giggs 963, Wayne Rooney 253, Bobby Charlton 249, Denis Law 237.
  • Dates for the counters (29 May 1968, 26 May 1999, 19 May 2013, 25 May 2024): the finals and the manager's last fixture.

Where a figure is contested — the shared shields, whether the Second Division counts — the site says so instead of picking a number quietly. That principle is stated on the cabinet page and applied throughout: if a number cannot be checked, it does not go on the page.

7. How AI was used, honestly

This project was made with an AI assistant, and the professor's criticism of the first version — that it was one prompt and no thinking — is a fair description of what that version was. So here is the split, in the plainest terms I can manage:

  • Mine: the idea, the scope, the decision to drop everything except three datasets, the choice of the programme/paper aesthetic, the page structure, which players and which moments made the cut, the editorial rule about checkable numbers, and the judgement calls in every paragraph that has an opinion in it.
  • Shared: the implementation. I described the components I wanted, the assistant wrote a first draft of the CSS and the JavaScript, and I read, tested and rewrote it — the chart's tooltip positioning, the XI builder's eligibility rules and the quiz's scoring all went through several versions because the first ones were wrong.
  • Verified by me, not assumed: every number in data.js is checked against the sources above. A generated page that looks right and says 71 trophies is worse than no page.

I am not claiming I typed every character. I am claiming I can explain every decision on it, which is the only version of this that survives being asked a question in class.

8. What I would do next

  • Add the pre-1992 league seasons to the same chart, so the Ferguson era can be compared with Busby's rather than just with what came after.
  • Make the XI builder check itself: warn when a chosen midfield has no defensive player, and let two XIs be compared side by side.
  • Add a proper data test — a small script in the repository that asserts 20 league titles, 13 FA Cups, 68 in total — so a typo in data.js fails loudly instead of shipping.
